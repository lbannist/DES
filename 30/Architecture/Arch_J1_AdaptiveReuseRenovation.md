# 🏚️ Architecture 12 — Unit J, Capstone Project J1: Adaptive Reuse & Renovation
### *Design Within What Already Exists*

---

> **Core Idea:** Too often developers will bulldoze and erase what was there, along with all of its history, to make a cookie-cutter building they build 100s of. Sometimes it is important to keep the history of the building and add on to it, make an addition, or perform an interior remodel — because a new building in an established neighbourhood will not blend in and appear like it has always been there.

## Phase 1 — Building Type Selection and Background Research

### What You're Doing
You're choosing the type of building you want to reimagine. You don't need to find a specific building to measure, but you should choose a type that has recognizable and consistent physical characteristics — something you can research and understand before you start designing.

### Choosing Your Building Type
Good choices for this project have a few things in common: they're common enough that you can find examples and research their typical characteristics, and they're interesting enough that you can imagine them becoming something completely different. Consider:

- **Strip mall unit / small commercial block** — wide open floor plates, parking-focused sites
- **Warehouse or light industrial** — high ceilings, large structural bays, loading access
- **Single-storey school or community hall** — large open rooms, institutional character
- **Motel** — repetitive unit structure, exterior corridor access, abundant parking
- **Church or place of worship** — dramatic interior volumes, often in residential areas
- **Fire hall** — large apparatus bays, mix of residential and operational spaces

### Background Research
Once you've chosen your type, research its typical physical characteristics. You need to understand what you're working with before you can redesign it. Investigate:
- Standard structural bay widths and column spacing
- Typical ceiling heights *(this is huge — high ceilings open up many possibilities)*
- What the exterior is usually made of *(masonry, metal cladding, tilt-up concrete?)*
- How the building typically sits on its site *(setbacks, parking, loading)*
- The construction era and any character features worth preserving

### Precedent Research
Find two or three real-world examples of your building type being successfully adapted for a new use. For each precedent, note what the building was originally, what it became, what physical features were kept, and what was changed. Include images where possible.

> 💡 **Tip:** Search terms like *"warehouse to loft conversion"*, *"church to residential"*, or *"school adaptive reuse"* will return great examples. [ArchDaily.com](https://www.archdaily.com) is an excellent resource for high-quality architectural projects with photos and drawings.

> 🎓 **Grade 12 Upgrade — Read It Like a Brief (Unit G skill):** Before you settle on a building type, treat your own research the way you treated the BILD package in G1. Annotate what's a hard physical constraint (bay width, ceiling height, structural grid) versus a soft characteristic (era, style, finish) — you'll use this same split again in Phase 3.

### ✅ Phase 1 Deliverables
- [ ] Written building type selection rationale
- [ ] Building type profile *(physical characteristics, structural system, typical dimensions)*
- [ ] Precedent research — two to three examples with images and notes

## Phase 2 — New Use Investigation, Community Need & Code Research

### What You're Doing
The most important question in adaptive reuse is *why*. You need to identify a genuine community or human need that your reimagined building will serve, then start understanding what the Alberta Building Code requires once that use changes.

### Finding a Real Need
Think about your community. What is missing, underserved, or desperately needed? Some ideas that have worked well in real adaptive reuse projects:

- **Affordable housing** — converting a motel or office building into apartment units
- **Artist live-work studios** — warehouse to mixed residential and studio space
- **Neighbourhood food hub** — strip mall to market hall with food vendors and community kitchen
- **Youth drop-in centre** — former school to youth programming and recreation space
- **Coworking and maker space** — industrial building to shared workspace with tools and studios
- **Childcare facility** — commercial building to licensed daycare with outdoor play area
- **Transitional or supportive housing** — motel to small-unit housing with support services

### The Fit Test
Your new use needs to make sense for the building type you've chosen. Ask yourself whether the building's size fits the scale of this use, whether the structural characteristics *(ceiling heights, bay widths, floor loads)* support what you're imagining, and whether the site location is appropriate.

### Client and User Profile
Define who will use the building after it's been reimagined. Be specific. Understanding your users drives your design decisions the same way a client profile does in a new-build project.

### Alberta Building Code Research *(🎓 Grade 12 Upgrade — Unit H skill)*
A change of use isn't just a design decision, it's a code decision. Using what you learned in Unit H, research and cite the **specific Alberta Building Code sections** that apply to your change of use. At minimum, address:
- **Occupancy classification change** — what group is your building moving from and to, and what does that trigger?
- **Egress** — number of exits, travel distance, and whether your new occupant load changes the requirements
- **Fire separation** — what separations are required between the new occupancy and anything adjacent (especially important if only part of the building is being converted)
- **Accessibility and plumbing/washroom counts** for the new occupancy

This isn't a paragraph of vague impressions — cite actual section numbers, the way you did in your Unit H code research.

### ✅ Phase 2 Deliverables
- [ ] New use selection and written justification *(why this use, why this building type?)*
- [ ] User / client profile
- [ ] Written needs analysis
- [ ] **Alberta Building Code compliance notes citing specific applicable sections** *(occupancy, egress, fire separation, accessibility)*

## Phase 3 — Existing Conditions Plan, Structural Read & Constraint Mapping

### What You're Doing
Before you design anything new, you need to fully understand what you're working with — including how it stands up. This phase documents the existing building accurately, reads its basic structural system, and then analyzes both for constraints and opportunities.

### Existing Conditions Floor Plan
Draw a scaled floor plan of the building as it currently exists. Include all structural walls and columns *(these likely can't move)*, existing door and window openings, any fixed elements — stairs, mechanical rooms, loading docks, washrooms — overall dimensions and ceiling heights noted, and a north arrow.

### Basic Structural Read *(🎓 Grade 12 Upgrade — Unit H skill)*
Using the same structural thinking from Unit H's foundation/framing challenge, identify and note on your existing conditions plan: the likely structural system (wood frame, steel frame, masonry bearing wall, tilt-up concrete), the approximate column/bay spacing, and which existing walls are load-bearing versus non-structural. You don't need a full structural model — you need to know, and show, which walls you actually *can't* move.

### Constraint and Opportunity Map
Take your existing conditions plan and annotate it to identify three categories:

| Must Stay | Could Change | Opportunities |
|---|---|---|
| Structural walls | Non-structural partitions | High ceilings for mezzanines |
| Load-bearing columns | Window locations and sizes | North skylights for studios |
| Floor slab | Entry points | Large bays for open plans |
| Foundation | Interior finishes | Loading dock as covered entry |
| Exterior envelope *(usually)* | Mechanical and plumbing | Exposed structure as character |

> 💡 **Tip:** The best adaptive reuse projects don't fight their constraints — they celebrate them. A column grid that can't move becomes a rhythm that organizes the new floor plan.

### ✅ Phase 3 Deliverables
- [ ] Existing conditions floor plan *(scaled, with all structural and fixed elements)*
- [ ] Basic structural read noted on the plan *(system type, bay spacing, load-bearing walls identified)*
- [ ] Annotated constraint and opportunity map
- [ ] Written analysis of key constraints and the opportunities they create

## Phase 4 — Design Brief

### What You're Doing
With your building understood and your new use established, you're ready to write a formal design brief. This document brings everything together and becomes the reference point for every design decision that follows.

### What Your Design Brief Should Include

**Project Summary** — one paragraph describing what this project is: what the building was, what it will become, and why this transformation matters.

**Community Need** — a clear statement of the human or community need your design will serve, supported by reasoning about why this need exists.

**User / Client Description** — who will use this building, who the client is, and what their priorities are.

**Constraints Summary** — key physical and structural constraints inherited from the existing building.

**Code and Zoning Notes** — the code sections identified in Phase 2, and any zoning issues relevant to the change of use.

**Design Intentions** — two or three clear statements about what this project is trying to achieve.

**Building Program** — a room-by-room list of all spaces required, with approximate sizes and key functional relationships, checked against your existing floor plate.

### ✅ Phase 4 Deliverables
- [ ] Complete written design brief
- [ ] Building program *(room list with approximate areas and relationships)*
- [ ] Feasibility check — does the program fit the existing building footprint?

## Phase 5 — Concept Development

### What You're Doing
Now you start generating ideas for how the new use will inhabit the existing building. Same rule as any design phase: **volume before refinement.**

### What Your Thumbnails Should Explore
- Different circulation strategies — where do people enter, how do they move through the building?
- Different ways of handling the relationship between old and new — what gets exposed and celebrated versus covered or removed?
- How outdoor space is activated
- How the exterior changes to signal the new identity while respecting the existing shell
- Where the new use requires the most significant intervention versus where it can work with what's already there

> 💡 **Tip:** Annotate your thumbnails with notes about what each approach preserves, what it changes, and why.

### Evaluate Before You Commit *(🎓 Grade 12 Upgrade — Unit G skill)*
Just like you did in G2, don't just pick your favourite. Build a short comparison scoring each thumbnail direction against your design brief's stated priorities (community need fit, structural feasibility, code feasibility, cost/complexity of intervention). Select one direction and write a rationale that explicitly references that comparison.

### ✅ Phase 5 Deliverables
- [ ] Minimum 4–6 annotated thumbnail concept sketches
- [ ] A comparison of thumbnail directions against your brief's priorities
- [ ] Written concept selection rationale

## Phase 6 — Design Development & Professional Documentation

### What You're Doing
With your concept selected, you develop it into a complete, professionally formatted set of architectural drawings. In an adaptive reuse project, your drawings need to clearly communicate what is existing, what is being removed, and what is new.

### New Floor Plan
**Scale: 1:50 or 1:100.** Draw your new floor plan clearly distinguishing new construction from existing elements *(heavy lines for new, lighter for existing, or different colours)*. All rooms labeled and dimensioned. Verify your building program is fully accounted for, and that egress paths from Phase 2 are visible in the layout.

### Elevations *(minimum 2 — front and one other)*
Show how the exterior has been transformed. Material callouts are required.

### Building Section
**Scale: 1:50 or 1:100.** Show interior ceiling heights, floor levels, and how the existing structural system has been worked with or around.

### Detail Drawing *(🎓 Grade 12 Upgrade — Unit H skill)*
Pick one condition in your design that deserves a closer look — a new wall meeting the existing structure, a new opening cut through an existing bearing wall, or a stair if your design adds one — and draft it as a **wall section or detail at a larger scale**, the same way you did in Unit H. This is the drawing that shows you actually understand how old and new meet, not just that they do.

### Demolition / Change Plan
This drawing shows what is being removed, retained, and new — typically using three line types or colours, with a legend.

### Professional Sheet Set *(🎓 Grade 12 Upgrade — Unit H skill)*
Assemble your floor plan, elevations, section, detail, and demolition plan into a **coordinated CD-style sheet set**: a consistent title block on every sheet, a logical sheet numbering system (e.g., A0.1 cover, A1.1 existing plan, A2.1 new plan, A3.1 elevations, A4.1 section, A5.1 detail, A6.1 demo plan), and a cover sheet with a drawing index — following the same standard you set up in Unit H.

### ✅ Phase 6 Deliverables
- [ ] New floor plan distinguishing existing and new elements
- [ ] Minimum two elevations showing the transformation
- [ ] Building section
- [ ] One wall section or detail drawing at a larger scale
- [ ] Demolition / change plan
- [ ] All drawings assembled into a coordinated sheet set with title block, sheet numbering, and a cover sheet/index

## Phase 7 — Physical Model, Digital Presentation & Portfolio

### 3D Printed Model — Part A: Floor Plan with Two-Colour Walls

Your physical model for this project tells a **before-and-after story**, made visible through two different wall colours.

#### 🎓 Grade 12 Upgrade — Export & Print Pipeline (Unit I skill)
Model your two wall sets directly in Revit at 1:1, using the same reference point for both sets so they line up. **Export each set directly from Revit as an STL or OBJ**, exactly as you did in Unit I — no need to rebuild geometry in a separate app. Bring both files into **Elegoo Slicer or Orca Slicer**, scale each to **1:100**, and check wall thickness (minimum 3–4 mm) and any thin overhangs before slicing, just as you checked in your Unit I print-prep challenge.

| Existing Walls *(Retained)* | New Walls *(Added)* |
|---|---|
| Print in grey or concrete tone | Print in white or warm tone |
| Represents the original structure | Represents your design intervention |
| Includes all load-bearing elements identified in Phase 3 | Includes new partitions and enclosures |

Prepare a clean printed floor plan base at the same 1:100 scale, distinguishing existing from new with shading or line colour and a printed legend, mounted on foam core or MDF.

> 💡 **Tip:** If a wall in your design has a new opening cut through an existing wall, show that opening in the model — it communicates the renovation logic better than any drawing can.

### 3D Printed Model — Part B: Scaled Site Model

Print your site plan at 1:200 or 1:500 on a rigid base, showing any site changes — new entry sequence, parking reconfiguration, added landscaping. Print a simplified massing model of the renovated building at the same scale, exported the same way as Part A. Consider printing simplified adjacent buildings for context.

### Advanced Twinmotion Presentation *(🎓 Grade 12 Upgrade — Unit I skill)*

Where the original version of this project ended with a photo series of the physical model, your capstone version pairs that model with a **digital presentation built in Twinmotion**, at the advanced level from Unit I. Choose one:

- **A before-and-after render set** — a day and a night render of the exterior and one key interior space, clearly showing the transformation, or
- **A short cinematic walkthrough or VR-ready sequence** through the reimagined building, from arrival to the key new space

### 📸 Before and After Documentation

Because this is a renovation project, still document your **physical** model assembly as a three-stage photograph series:

| Stage | What to Photograph |
|---|---|
| **Stage 1 — Before** | The printed floor plan base before any walls are placed |
| **Stage 2 — Existing** | After the existing *(grey)* walls are placed |
| **Stage 3 — After** | After the new *(white)* walls are added |

### Final Presentation
Your critique presentation tells the story of a building's transformation: what it was, what need it now serves, what the constraints were (physical, structural, and code), and how your design worked with them. Present your physical model and your Twinmotion output together — the model shows the intervention, the render/walkthrough shows the experience.

### Portfolio Compilation
Your portfolio includes all phase work from building type research through the final sheet set, model photographs, and Twinmotion output. Include a written reflection addressing what the existing building gave you to work with, what surprised you about the code and structural constraints, and how your project serves both its users and the broader value of adaptive reuse as a sustainable building practice.

- [ ] 3D printed two-colour wall model, exported from Revit and sliced at 1:100, on printed floor plan base
- [ ] Scaled site model with renovated building massing
- [ ] Before and after model photograph series *(3 stages)*
- [ ] Advanced Twinmotion presentation *(render set or walkthrough)*
- [ ] Final critique presentation
- [ ] Complete portfolio *(all phase work)*

## 🔧 Common Problems and Fixes

| Problem | Likely Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Two wall sets don't fit together at junctions | Modeled in separate files with misaligned origins | Model both sets in the same Revit project using the same shared reference point before exporting |
| STL export is too dense or fails to slice cleanly | Full model detail exported instead of simplified massing | Simplify geometry before export — remove unnecessary detail, keep wall/opening geometry only |
| Walls too fragile and break on removal from bed | Wall thickness below 2–3 mm at 1:100 | Increase wall thickness in Revit before export; use a brim for bed adhesion |
| Openings don't align with floor plan print | Scale error between exported model and printed base | Re-verify scale in the slicer before printing; reprint base before gluing walls |
| Colours look too similar | Filament choices not contrasting enough | Use high contrast: dark grey vs. white, or natural vs. bright white |
| Print warps or lifts off bed | Bed adhesion issue | Use brim setting in Elegoo/Orca Slicer; ensure bed is level and clean |
