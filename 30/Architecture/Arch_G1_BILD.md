# Architectural Design 12 – Brief Analysis

## Unit G: Competition Design Challenge (BILD-style) — Challenge G1

**Time:** 1 Week (3–4 Class Periods)
**Due Date:** *[insert due date]*

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this assignment students will:

- Read and interpret a real competition package the way a practicing design team would before starting design
- Extract a complete client program (spatial list, square footages, target buyer/market)
- Separate hard constraints (site, zoning, budget, code, sustainability targets) from soft preferences (style, "nice-to-haves")
- Identify how the jury will actually score the entry, and what that means for where design effort should go

---

## 💭 Production Question

### If you only had one hour with the client, what would you need to ask them — and where does the package already answer that for you?

---

## Checkpoint 1 — First Read & Annotation (Day 1)

**✏️ Read the Whole Package Before You Design Anything**

1. Read the assigned past BILD (or similar) competition package start to finish without sketching anything yet.
2. Annotate the package (digitally or on a printout) highlighting: program requirements, site information, code/regulatory notes, and anything unclear.
3. Flag at least three items that would need clarification if you could ask the client a question.

**Requirements**

- Fully annotated package (digital markup or scanned printout)
- At least three flagged clarification questions

---

## Checkpoint 2 — Program & Constraint Extraction (Day 2)

**✏️ Build Your Program Document**

1. Extract a room-by-room program list: spaces, approximate square footages, and any relationships called for (e.g., "ensuite off primary bedroom," "mudroom adjacent to garage").
2. List all hard constraints separately: lot dimensions/setbacks, height restrictions, applicable code requirements, budget or cost targets, sustainability or accessibility requirements.
3. List soft preferences separately: style direction, "wow factor" asks, optional upgrades.
4. State the total target square footage and check it against the site's buildable envelope.

**Requirements**

- A complete program list with square footages
- Hard constraints and soft preferences clearly separated
- A stated buildable envelope check (does the program plausibly fit the site?)

---

## Checkpoint 3 — Scoring Breakdown & Precedent Research (Day 3)

**✏️ Reverse-Engineer the Jury**

1. Locate the competition's judging/scoring criteria (or, if not provided, infer a reasonable weighting from the package's emphasis) and rank which categories carry the most points.
2. Research two precedent projects (built or past competition entries) that respond to a similar program or site type, and note one idea from each worth borrowing.
3. Summarize, in your own words, the three things your design absolutely cannot get wrong to score well.

**Requirements**

- A scoring breakdown or inferred priority ranking
- Two precedent projects with one takeaway noted for each
- A short list of the top three "cannot get wrong" priorities

---

## Checkpoint 4 — Reflection

**✏️ Answer:**

1. What surprised you about the program once you actually broke it down?
2. Which constraint do you expect to be the hardest to design around, and why?
3. Based on the scoring breakdown, where should you spend the most design time in G2–G3?
4. What is the single clarifying question you'd most want answered before starting concept design?

## 📊 Assessment Criteria

**Package Comprehension (25%)**

- Thorough, accurate annotation
- Meaningful clarification questions identified

**Program & Constraint Extraction (35%)**

- Complete, accurate program list with square footages
- Hard constraints and soft preferences correctly separated
- Buildable envelope check completed

**Scoring & Precedent Research (20%)**

- Reasonable, well-justified scoring priority ranking
- Two relevant precedents with clear takeaways

**Reflection (20%)**

- Thoughtful, specific answers connecting analysis to next steps

---

## 💡 Key Lesson

Every competition is won or lost before the first line is drawn — the teams that read the brief like a client conversation, not a checklist, are the ones whose concepts actually answer the real question being asked.
