## 💭 Production Question

### What makes a shot feel cinematic?

A cinematic shot is a carefully planned piece of visual storytelling.

Unlike a scene, a shot is a single uninterrupted camera view.

A movie, television show, or animated film may contain hundreds or even thousands of individual shots.

Professional animators rarely create entire films at once. Instead, they focus on making each shot as strong as possible.

A good cinematic shot:

- Directs the audience's attention
- Creates mood and atmosphere
- Reveals important information
- Uses composition effectively
- Supports the story

Reference: [What Is a Cinematic Shot? A Lesson for Motion Designers](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/what-is-a-cinematic-shot-a-lesson-for-motion-designers)

---

## Understanding the Difference: Shot vs. Scene

| Shot | Scene |
|---|---|
| One continuous camera view | Multiple shots combined together |
| Usually lasts a few seconds | May last several minutes |
| Focuses on one idea or moment | Advances a larger section of the story |
| Built around a single camera setup | Often contains many camera angles |

**Example:** A character entering a mysterious cave might be a *scene*. The camera slowly pushing toward the cave entrance while strange lights appear inside would be a *shot*.

For this assignment, focus on creating one strong shot rather than an entire scene.

## Assignment Overview

Create a cinematic shot that feels like it belongs in a larger film, game, or animated production.

The shot should communicate a clear mood, reveal information, or build tension.

The audience should understand where to look and why the moment matters.

## Checkpoint 1 — Shot Analysis (Day 1)

**📖 Find 2–3 examples of strong cinematic shots** from films, games, or animated shorts (a single memorable moment, not a full scene).

**✏️ Answer the following in a short written response, using your examples as reference:**

1. What is the focus of the shot?
2. Where does your eye go first?
3. How does the camera move?
4. What mood is created?

## Checkpoint 2 — Shot Planning (Day 1)

**✏️ Write a Shot Summary (3–5 sentences)** describing:

- What the audience sees
- What the audience learns
- Why this moment matters

**✏️ Create a Shot Composition Sketch** showing:

- Main subject
- Camera position
- Foreground elements
- Midground elements
- Background elements

**✏️ Write a Camera Plan** describing:

- Starting camera position
- Ending camera position
- Camera movement type

Examples: Pan, Zoom, Push-In, Pull-Out, Tracking Shot

## Checkpoint 3 — Production Planning (Day 1–2)

**✏️ Identify where your production time should be spent.** Professional animators focus their effort on the elements the audience will actually notice.

Examples:

| High Priority Elements | Low Priority Elements |

| Key Story Object | Minor Details |

## Checkpoint 4 — Animation Production (Days 2–4)

**✏️ Build your shot in Krita, using your Shot Summary, Composition Sketch, Camera Plan, and Priority Plan from Checkpoints 2–3 as your guide.**

**Requirements**

Length:

- 10–15 seconds

Must include:

- One uninterrupted shot
- One camera movement
- One focal point
- One clear story moment
- At least three previously learned techniques (see the list in "Building on Everything So Far" above)

**Production Limits**

Maximum:

- 2 major actions
- 2 camera movements

Students should focus on quality rather than quantity. The goal is to create a professional-looking shot, not a complete story.

Record their responses.
