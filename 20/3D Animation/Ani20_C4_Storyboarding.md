# 3D Animation 11 – Storyboarding & Animatic with Grease Pencil

## Unit 1: Character Refinement, Storyboarding & Animation Principles — Challenge C5 (Unit Capstone)

**Time:** 1 Week (3–4 Class Periods)
**Due Date:** *[insert due date]*

*Builds on Challenges C1–C3.*

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this assignment students will:

- Thumbnail multiple shot options before committing to a final storyboard
- Use camera shot types (wide, medium, close-up) and staging with intent
- Build a working animatic directly in Blender using Grease Pencil
- Combine planning skills with the character work from Challenges C1–C3

This is the unit capstone — your storyboard should plan a short sequence featuring the character you've refined all unit.

---

## 💭 Production Question

### How much of a story can you tell before a single frame is fully animated?

---

## Checkpoint 1 — Thumbnailing & Shot List (Day 1)

**📖 Thumbnail Multiple Options**

1. Write a one-sentence description of a short scene (5–15 seconds) featuring your character — something simple, like reacting to something, picking something up, or walking into a new space.
2. Break that scene into 3–5 shots (a "shot" is one continuous camera view — e.g., shot 1: wide shot of the room, shot 2: close-up of the character's face).
3. For each shot, draw 2–3 quick thumbnail options (tiny, rough sketches, a few seconds each) exploring different camera angles or framing choices.
4. Circle your favorite thumbnail for each shot and briefly note why you chose it over the alternatives.

**Questions to answer:**

1. What's the one-sentence version of the scene you're planning?
2. For each shot, which thumbnail did you choose, and why?

---

## Checkpoint 2 — Grease Pencil Storyboard Drawing (Days 1–2)

**✏️ Draw Your Storyboard in Blender**

1. Add a Grease Pencil object (Shift + A → Grease Pencil → Blank) for each shot, or use one Grease Pencil object with separate layers/keyframes per shot — check which approach your teacher prefers.
2. For each shot, set up a camera at roughly the angle/framing you chose in Checkpoint 1.
3. Draw a rough storyboard panel directly in the camera view using Grease Pencil's Draw Mode — you don't need full detail, just enough to show composition, character position, and staging.
4. Label each shot with its shot type (wide, medium, close-up) and a one-line description of the action.
5. Make sure each shot's staging clearly shows where your character is and what they're doing/reacting to.

**Requirements**

- All 3–5 shots drawn as Grease Pencil storyboard panels
- Each shot clearly labeled with shot type and action description
- Staging in each panel clearly communicates the character's position and action

---

## Checkpoint 3 — Animatic Assembly (Days 2–3)

**✏️ Build Your Animatic**

1. Arrange your storyboard shots in sequence on Blender's timeline, giving each shot a rough duration based on how long that beat of action should take.
2. Use the camera/scene switching tools (multiple camera markers, or the Video Sequence Editor if your class uses it) to cut between shots at the correct frames.
3. Play back the full animatic and check the pacing — does each shot last the right amount of time, or does something feel rushed or too slow?
4. Adjust shot durations based on that playback check.
5. If time allows, add simple placeholder sound (a clap, a footstep sound, dialogue scratch) to help sell the timing — optional but encouraged.

**Requirements**

- All shots sequenced with intentional durations
- Playback pacing checked and adjusted at least once
- Cuts between shots are clean, not accidental jumps

---

## Checkpoint 4 — Peer Critique (Day 3)

**✏️ Peer Critique**

1. Play your animatic for a peer group without additional explanation.
2. Ask: is the story/action clear? Does anything feel confusing or too fast/slow?
3. Ask specifically about the shot choices — does the camera work help tell the story, or get in the way?
4. Note at least two specific pieces of feedback and how you'll address them before final submission.

---

## Checkpoint 5 — Reflection (Day 4)

**✏️ Answer:**

1. Which shot was hardest to plan, and why?
2. How did thumbnailing multiple options before committing change your final choices?
3. What did building the animatic reveal about your pacing that the storyboard panels alone didn't show?
4. How did your Challenge C4 walk/run cycle work (or other animation principles) influence how you planned this sequence?
5. What's one thing you'll do differently next time you storyboard a scene?

## 📊 Assessment Criteria

**Thumbnailing & Planning (20%)**

- Multiple thumbnail options genuinely explored per shot, not just one option per shot
- Clear reasoning given for the chosen thumbnail over the alternatives
- Scene broken into a sensible number of shots for the story being told
- One-sentence scene description is clear and specific

**Storyboard Panels (25%)**

- All shots clearly drawn with readable staging
- Shot types used with intent (variety of wide/medium/close-up where appropriate)
- Character position and action clearly communicated in each panel
- Panels are legible and organized, even if rough

**Animatic Pacing (30%)**

- Shot durations feel intentional and support the story's pacing
- Playback was actually checked and adjusted, not left as a first guess
- Cuts between shots are clean and purposeful
- Animatic overall communicates the story clearly without needing extra explanation

**Critique Response (5%)**

- Evidence peer feedback was genuinely considered and applied
- At least two specific pieces of feedback documented
- Clear explanation of what was changed as a result
- Feedback addressed before final submission

**Reflection (20%)**

- Thoughtful, specific reflection on the process
- Honest identification of the hardest shot to plan and why
- Clear connection drawn to animation principles from Challenge C4
- Concrete idea for improving the storyboarding process next time

---

## 💡 Key Lesson

A storyboard's job is to solve story and staging problems cheaply, before any expensive animation work begins. This is the single most direct skill connection to the Skills Alberta 3D Animation competition, where storyboarding is explicitly judged — the habits built here matter well beyond this one assignment.
